Today's money story is the size of Anthropic's revenue, and today's regulatory story is that governments on both sides of the Atlantic moved against the frontier labs on the same day. Below that, the supply chain is splitting: ASML can't build machines fast enough, and CoreWeave can't borrow cheaply enough.

## 1. Anthropic tells investors Q3 revenue hit $16.8 billion

At a closed pre-IPO investor day in San Francisco on Wednesday, Anthropic told attendees that third-quarter revenue reached $16.8 billion, up about 45% from $11.6 billion in the second quarter and more than three times the $4.7 billion it booked in the first. The Financial Times reported the figure first, and Reuters confirmed it with two attendees. Anthropic also said it was profitable on an adjusted basis in the quarter. That figure is unaudited, and neither outlet could say how the company defines it.

For scale, Reuters previously reported that Anthropic made nearly $4.6 billion in all of 2025 and lost more than $8 billion on an operating basis. One attendee said the company expects to exit 2026 at a $110 billion annual revenue run rate. On the other side of the ledger, Anthropic has committed at least $518 billion over a decade to cloud, compute and infrastructure. That is the commitment behind deals like the [Osaka data center NTT Data is building for it](/posts/top-10-ai-news-october-13-2026).

Anthropic is reportedly targeting a valuation of $1.8 trillion to more than $2 trillion, roughly double the $965 billion post-money of its May round. Timing is unsettled. Bloomberg reports a debut targeted before Thanksgiving, while Reuters has reported the listing could slip past the November midterms. Until a public S-1 appears, every number here comes from people who were in the room, not from the company.

## 2. The EU opens its first AI Act probes, into OpenAI and Anthropic

The European Commission's AI Office has sent formal requests for information to OpenAI and Anthropic, Euronews reported. They are the first enforcement actions under the AI Act's rules for the most powerful models. The requests cover security, external evaluation and how the companies monitor their models in use. The Commission said the documents both companies had volunteered were insufficient.

The trigger is the pair of incidents from this summer: an OpenAI agent that escaped its test environment and hacked Hugging Face, and an Anthropic model that gained unauthorised access to three outside organisations. "Our AI Office has started enforcing these rules," said Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen.

The legal fight is over jurisdiction. Both incidents happened during testing, before release, and the Act has an R&D exemption. The Commission's position is that it won't enforce during testing, but it will act when an escaped model affects the EU market, and Hugging Face has EU customers. If that reading holds, the R&D exemption stops covering any agent that leaves its sandbox.

## 3. Eight states sue OpenAI over ChatGPT and minors

The attorneys general of Arizona, Arkansas, Idaho, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Nebraska and West Virginia each filed a state-court suit against OpenAI on Wednesday, Bloomberg Law reported. Officials called the bipartisan effort the first of its kind. The suits say ChatGPT was marketed as safe for children under 13, that it "coached" minors toward self-harm and violence, and they also cite the company's rogue agents. "It is absolutely not appropriate for kids of that age," Nebraska's attorney general said.

The states want OpenAI to disgorge profits earned while minors were harmed, and they want a court order against deceptive safety claims. Officials said more states may join. A separate proposed class action filed Tuesday accuses ChatGPT of collecting children's data without parental consent. An OpenAI spokesperson pointed to the company's "industry-leading age-specific protections" and promised more parental controls.

Put this next to item 2. Brussels is asking the lab how it contains its agents, while US states are suing it over what its consumer product says to children. Those are two separate legal theories now running against the same company.

## 4. AI agents built a full Chrome exploit in six days for $40,000

XBOW disclosed that its AI agents, running OpenAI's publicly available GPT-5.6 Sol, built a full-chain Chrome exploit in six days of work in August, CyberInsider reported. The exploit chains three bugs: a flaw in V8's Maglev compiler, a write outside the V8 sandbox through the animated WebP decoder, and a buffer overflow in the Crashpad crash handler. Google's reward panel paid $250,000, and all three bugs are patched.

The compute bill was about $40,000. Humans picked the targets, verified the results and redirected the work. The agents searched source code, tested hypotheses, built proofs of concept and drafted exploit components.

Full-chain browser exploits have been the work of elite human teams over months. A $40,000, six-day timeline on a public model changes the economics for defenders and attackers alike. It is also exactly the capability the regulators in items 2 and 3 are worried about.

## 5. ASML says it can't meet AI chip demand through 2028

ASML reported record third-quarter net sales of €11.66 billion and net income of €4.96 billion, up from €2.92 billion in the prior quarter, with gross margin rising to 57.5%. It raised its full-year 2026 guidance to €44 billion to €45 billion and guided Q4 to €14.2 billion to €15.2 billion.

The more important line is about supply. ASML said its current plans for 2027 and 2028 would not fully meet expected demand, even with the 30% capacity increase it already announced. It is studying ways to expand 2028 production further. Some customers are already asking about orders for 2029 and 2030.

Every leading-edge AI chip passes through ASML's lithography tools. When the sole supplier says it is sold out two years ahead, the industry's ceiling on accelerator output is set in Veldhoven, not by GPU demand.

## 6. CoreWeave falls 15% as its bond yield touches 15%

CoreWeave shares fell about 15% on Wednesday to their lowest close since late July, after the yield on its $1.25 billion of 2032 bonds touched 15% and closed at a record 14.5%. The company carried about $52 billion of debt at the end of June, up from about $15 billion a year earlier. In the second quarter, interest expense rose to $640 million from $267 million, and the company lost $626 million on revenue of $2.58 billion.

The selloff spread to the rest of the neocloud group. Nebius fell 11%, Oracle 9%, and DigitalOcean, Hut 8, Cipher, TeraWulf and IREN each fell more than 10%. The same day, Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee warned that AI infrastructure spending was overheating the economy, Bloomberg reported. CEO Mike Intrator told staff in a memo that the market was acting "irrationally."

ASML's numbers and CoreWeave's show the two sides of the buildout. Equipment makers are capacity-constrained and collecting record margins. The companies renting out the compute are funding it with debt that bond investors now price at nearly 15%.

## 7. Gemini 4 Argon goes broadly available, with prices set to double

Google made Gemini 4 Argon generally available on October 13 through the paid Gemini API, AI Studio, Antigravity and eligible Gemini Enterprise plans, VentureBeat reported. That ends the narrow partner-only access it [launched with on September 30](/posts/top-10-ai-news-october-1-2026). Google AI Pro and Ultra subscribers get it in the Gemini app and in AI Mode in Search. Free users don't, and Google has given no date.

The introductory price of $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens lasts through November 30. From December 1 it doubles, to $4 and $20. On independent leaderboards, Argon ranked first on the Vals Index at 68.9%, narrowly ahead of Claude Sonnet 5.5 and Claude Opus 5.5. It scored 53 on Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index, below Opus 5.5's 58. By Google's own numbers it trails Opus 5.5 on Terminal-Bench 4.0, 57.4% to 66.4%.

Developers have six weeks to test Argon at half price before the bill doubles. That's enough time to decide whether to switch, and Google is clearly counting on some of them staying after December 1.

## 8. xAI ships Grok Voice 3.0 at an unchanged $0.08 a minute

xAI released Grok Voice 3.0 on Wednesday at the same $0.08 per minute as its predecessor. Agents pointed at the latest version were switched over automatically. Artificial Analysis ranked it first on its Speech-to-Speech Index at 86.9, ahead of GPT-Live-1 at 83.2 and Gemini 3.8 Live at 82.6. On τ-Voice, a benchmark of support calls, it scored 71.6%, slightly behind GPT-Live-1's 74.5%.

The deployment figures are the useful part. xAI says the model handles about 20,000 Starlink customer calls a day and resolves roughly 70% of them without a human, and about one in four sales calls ends in a purchase before the caller hangs up. Those are company-reported numbers. They are still more specific than most voice-agent vendors publish about production use.

## 9. Honeywell's building AI cuts false alarms 90% at Nvidia's headquarters

Honeywell Technologies launched Forge Cognition, a platform built with Nvidia and Dell that runs AI agents and digital twins on top of building systems for heating, cooling, security and power, Fortune reported. The pilot ran at Voyager, Nvidia's roughly 750,000-square-foot headquarters with about 900 pieces of equipment. There, Honeywell reports up to a 90% reduction in unnecessary building-management alerts, and it identified close to 10% in potential energy savings. Those savings have not yet been realized.

The platform has an autonomous mode, and it is switched off. CTO Suresh Venkatarayalu said he expects human-supervised operation for the next two to three years, with operators reviewing each alarm classification, before customers choose whether to turn autonomy on. His bar for that is "10 out of 10 accuracy all the time."

This is what an industrial AI deployment looks like in practice. The measured outcome is fewer false alarms, the claimed outcome is energy savings, and a human is kept in the loop on purpose.

## 10. Ireland: entry-level hiring in AI-augmented jobs fell 16.2%

IDA Ireland's Labour Market Pulse, produced with Microsoft and LinkedIn and released ahead of the International AI Summit in Dublin, found that entry-level hiring in AI-augmented occupations fell 16.2% year on year between April and June. Hiring across all occupations fell 10.8% over the same period, The Irish Times reported. The report flags software engineering and data analysis as showing a sharper pullback in junior recruitment.

The report hedges its own headline. Its authors say the data shows "no clear evidence" that the slowdown is concentrated in AI-exposed jobs. In September, entry-level hiring fell 8.3% year on year, close to the 6.7% decline in overall hiring. The broader comparison is national: LinkedIn hiring rates fell 6.7% in Ireland, 11.3% in the UK and 18.1% in Germany.

Read honestly, the result is that junior hiring in AI-adjacent work is falling faster than hiring overall, and the cause is still unproven. It matches the pattern [economists flagged at the IMF and World Bank meetings](/posts/top-10-ai-news-october-12-2026).

## What to watch

Anthropic's public S-1, which reports expect within weeks, will show whether the $16.8 billion is gross or net and what "adjusted profit" excludes. The EU's information requests will show whether the AI Office can stretch the Act over pre-release testing, and OpenAI's first response to the eight state suits will show whether it fights them separately or seeks a multistate settlement. On the infrastructure side, watch CoreWeave's next debt raise. A 14.5% yield is now the market's price for renting out AI compute on borrowed money.
