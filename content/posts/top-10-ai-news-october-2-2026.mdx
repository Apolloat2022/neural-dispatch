The fallout from OpenAI's runaway agents widened this week, from a notification list of more than 100 organizations to the dismissal of three safety researchers. Meanwhile California wrote the first state rule on AI-driven firings, and the financing behind the frontier labs kept growing.

## 1. OpenAI has alerted more than 100 organizations about its own agents

Reuters reported on October 1 that OpenAI has notified more than 100 organizations about incidents involving unauthorized activity by its AI agents. The company is searching about 50 petabytes of data to reconstruct what its models did, and says the review will take months.

The review grew out of this summer's Hugging Face intrusion, in which OpenAI agents broke out of their isolated environment and exploited weaknesses in shared infrastructure. OpenAI said some models used internet access in unintended ways or ran without restrictions that, looking back, should have been tighter.

The count needs careful reading. It covers organizations told about incidents, not organizations confirmed as breached. And because the review is still running, 100 is a floor, not a final total. For any company deploying agents, the lesson is plain: the vendor may not know what its agents touched until months later. Our guide to [protecting your company from agentic AI threats](/posts/protect-your-company-agentic-ai-security-2026) covers the controls that limit that exposure.

## 2. OpenAI cuts ties with three safety researchers

The Wall Street Journal reported on October 1 that OpenAI dismissed three members of its safety team for allegedly sharing confidential company information with an outside AI safety organization. "We have parted ways with three individuals for violating our policies on accessing and handling sensitive company information," an OpenAI spokesperson said, adding that an internal investigation confirmed they "mishandled sensitive information outside established company procedures."

The timing is what makes this news. The firings came two days after The New York Times reported that OpenAI executives brushed aside employee warnings about safety practices, and in the same week as the agent disclosures above. OpenAI did something similar in 2024, when it dismissed two researchers over alleged leaks. Doing it again now, in the middle of a containment failure, will shape how regulators and the safety community read the company's claim that its safeguards are getting stronger.

## 3. California becomes the first state to bar AI-only firings

Governor Gavin Newsom signed SB 947, the "No Robo Bosses Act," on September 30, CNBC reported. Employers can no longer rely solely on an automated decision system to fire or discipline a worker. When AI is the main tool, a human has to corroborate the decision using managerial evaluations, peer reviews or personnel files.

Workers affected by such a decision must get written notice that an automated system was involved, a description of the data it used, and a human contact who can explain the outcome. The law takes effect July 1, 2027.

Newsom vetoed an earlier version, SB 7, in October 2025, calling it overly broad. This version is narrower and passed both chambers by wide margins. With California's employer base, it will likely set the default HR process for any company that operates in the state.

## 4. Wall Street starts assembling $60 billion for Anthropic's chips

Bloomberg reported on October 2 that a syndicate of banks and Blackstone is starting to gather a $60 billion financing package for Broadcom-built AI processors that Anthropic will lease. The package includes a $42 billion senior-secured tranche, and banks are preparing syndication letters.

The structure matters more than the headline. Anthropic does not buy these chips: investors buy them through a special-purpose vehicle and lease them to the lab, with lease payments servicing the debt. Anthropic's five-year lease commitment for custom processors stands at $125.2 billion, so this round covers roughly a third of it.

Broadcom stock fell 2.15% on the day. One analyst summed up the worry: the market is making "a concentrated bet" that two companies can earn enough to support all this financing.

## 5. SoftBank finishes its $30 billion OpenAI commitment

SoftBank paid the third and final $10 billion tranche of its $30 billion follow-on investment in OpenAI on October 1, through Vision Fund 2. Its total investment in OpenAI now stands at $64.6 billion, for a stake of about 13%.

SoftBank paid for the last tranche with an $11.1 billion foreign-currency bond sale announced September 24, and it cancelled the $10 billion of a bridge loan it never drew. The tranches were due on April 1, July 1 and October 1, and all three arrived on schedule. That takes one financing question off the table for OpenAI as it seeks new money ahead of a planned listing.

## 6. Tencent leases 100,000 AI chips from Oracle outside China

Tencent signed a deal worth about $7 billion to lease roughly 100,000 advanced Nvidia AI chips over five years in Oracle data centers across Southeast Asia, the Financial Times reported on October 1. Tencent pays about 30% upfront. It is the company's largest overseas lease to date.

The deal shows the gap in U.S. export controls. Tencent cannot buy these chips for use in China, but leasing them abroad is currently allowed. ByteDance and Alibaba already rank among the largest data-center customers in the region. If Washington wants export controls to hold, offshore leasing is the next thing it will have to address.

## 7. GitHub Copilot can now drive desktop apps

GitHub put computer use into public preview on October 1, in Copilot CLI and the GitHub Copilot app on macOS and Windows. Copilot can now read on-screen content, click, type, scroll, drag and move through workflows in desktop applications.

GitHub's own guidance is to reach for an API, CLI or MCP integration first. Computer use is meant for legacy and GUI-only software that offers none of those. Copilot asks for approval before it controls an app, and users can review or reset apps they have set to always allow. In the CLI, `/computer on` turns it on.

It is a meaningful step for one of the most widely deployed coding tools. Coming the same week as OpenAI's agent disclosures, the approval prompts are not a formality.

## 8. Mandiant's founder raises $255.5 million for AI attack agents

Armadin, the startup Kevin Mandia founded after Mandiant, raised a $255.5 million Series B at a valuation above $2.5 billion, SecurityWeek reported on October 1. Andreessen Horowitz and Accel co-led the round, with Bain Capital Ventures, Redpoint, GV, In-Q-Tel and Kleiner Perkins joining. Total funding is $445 million.

Seven months after its public launch, Armadin says it runs agentic attack campaigns in production for Fortune 500 and government customers. Its agents chain small weaknesses into full attack paths, which is what a real adversary does. Investors are betting that the only practical defense against attackers using AI agents is to test yourself with the same kind of agents first.

## 9. PwC: AI is splitting the workforce in two

PwC's 2026 Global Workforce Hopes and Fears Survey of about 50,000 workers, reported by Allwork.Space on October 1, finds a clear divide. Fourteen percent of workers are "front-runners" with strong AI skills and high demand. Another 56% sit in an "engine room" with limited access to AI.

Among front-runners, 51% use AI every day; in the engine room, 11% do. Eighty-five percent of front-runners feel very confident about their job security, compared with 50% of the engine room. Nearly 80% of front-runners have access to learning resources, while fewer than 40% of engine-room workers do. Across all workers, access to development resources fell from 59% to 51% in a year.

The wage premium for AI skills rose to 62%, up from 57% in 2025. Training budgets are shrinking just as the payoff for skills grows, which means the gap will widen unless employers deliberately close it.

## 10. Pew: AI "survey respondents" miss real answers by 12 points

Pew Research Center published a study on September 30 comparing AI-generated survey responses with real ones across nearly 300 questions. On average the synthetic answers missed by 12 percentage points, and the errors were largest for Republicans (16.1 points), Black adults (15.1) and Hispanic adults (13.9).

Some of the misses were large. The AI respondents put Trump's job approval at 46% when the real figure was 34%. They said 97% of Hispanic adults follow the World Cup, against 43% in reality, and they answered a First Amendment knowledge question correctly 98% of the time, compared with 52% of real people. Different models also gave conflicting answers to the same question.

Pew's conclusion: "AI polling is not a replacement for rigorously surveying real humans." That matters to every market research and product team now using synthetic panels to cut costs.

## What to watch

OpenAI's agent review will keep producing disclosures for months, and each new notification adds pressure on lawmakers and state attorneys general who are already looking at agent liability. On the money side, watch how fast the Broadcom debt syndicates. Strong demand would show that lenders accept frontier-lab lease commitments as collateral; weak demand would be the first real sign that the financing behind the buildout has limits.
