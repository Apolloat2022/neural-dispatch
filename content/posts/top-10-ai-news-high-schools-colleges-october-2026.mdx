The fall 2026 semester is the first in which AI is built into the default tools nearly every student uses. Schools and universities are responding unevenly: some are signing campus-wide deals, some are switching features off, and most are still writing the rules. Here are the ten stories that defined the start of the school year.

## 1. Andreessen Horowitz opens an AI school as a college alternative

Andreessen Horowitz launched the Horowitz Andreessen Academy on September 22, a tuition-free San Francisco program for recent high school graduates and college dropouts who want to build AI products instead of going to university, Bloomberg reported.

The first cohort is about 50 students, chosen on "proof of work": what applicants have built, shipped or earned. There are no grades or exams. Students work on their own projects, take short practitioner-led courses and are offered compute credits, a travel budget and placements with partner companies. The ten founding partners are Anduril, Anthropic, Coinbase, Google, Meta, Nvidia, OpenAI, Palantir, Replit and Stripe.

The program is not accredited and grants no degree or transferable credit. A longer program is planned for 2028, and it may charge tuition comparable to elite private universities. CEO Gagan Biyani said the goal is to teach students how to build, which he called "the most important skill in the AI era."

For high school seniors weighing their options, it is the clearest sign yet that some of the industry's biggest backers see college as optional for AI careers.

## 2. Google puts Gemini in front of every K-12 student, and schools scramble

Google expanded Gemini in Google Classroom to students of all ages in August. The feature had previously been limited to students 18 and older. Students can now turn class materials into flashcards, practice quizzes and study guides, and a "Guided Learning" mode asks probing questions instead of handing over answers.

Administrators can disable Gemini by domain or user group, and districts that had already switched it off stay off. But Education Week reported that many schools were caught flat-footed. Michael Martin, principal of Plymouth High School in Ohio, said he learned about the change from a news story and disabled it because teachers "as a whole haven't been trained to use it." Robbie Torney of Common Sense Media said the rollout "defaults to adoption rather than permission."

The broader point: when the platform a district already runs ships AI to students by default, the decision about whether to adopt it shifts from the school board to the IT admin console.

## 3. Students are hiring lawyers to fight AI-cheating accusations

The Financial Times reported on October 3 that university students are increasingly retaining lawyers to challenge accusations of unauthorized AI use. One student spent more than £3,000 clearing her name by producing drafts and notes that proved she wrote her work. Lawyers say caseloads are rising because detection software is unreliable and university policies are inconsistent.

Some universities are already adjusting, treating minor AI misuse as poor academic practice rather than formal misconduct. The lesson for institutions is that a detector score is not evidence. Any penalty that can derail a student's career needs a process that would survive a lawyer's review.

## 4. Schools are buying AI tools with little evidence they work

NPR reported on September 28 that districts are spending real money on AI with almost no proof of results. Dayton, Ohio renewed a SchoolAI contract for $78,650. A New York district spent nearly $58,000 on a humanoid robot before community opposition halted the pilot. A Stanford review of more than 800 papers found "little solid evidence" to guide purchasing decisions.

Robin Lake of the Center on Reinventing Public Education called the market "very Wild West." Until vendors are required to show learning outcomes, districts should treat AI purchases as pilots with exit clauses, not multiyear commitments.

## 5. Provosts use AI every week, but only one in ten colleges has a strategy

Inside Higher Ed's annual survey of 376 chief academic officers, published September 23, found that roughly seven in ten provosts use AI in their own work at least weekly. Only about one in ten said their institution has a centralized AI strategy.

That gap matches what students report. The Digital Education Council's 2026 global survey of more than 45,000 students and faculty in 35 countries found that 88% of students and 77% of faculty use AI, yet 57% of students say they get inadequate guidance on AI in assessments. Leaders are using the tools personally, but institutional policy has not caught up.

## 6. Universities sign campus-wide ChatGPT Edu deals

Two major universities moved from pilots to institution-wide access in September. The University of Sydney announced on September 14 that all students and staff will get free, supported access to ChatGPT Edu, with university data excluded from model training. "It's our responsibility to graduate students who are well equipped for the modern world of work," said Vice-Chancellor Mark Scott.

On September 29, INSEAD rolled out ChatGPT Edu across its campuses in France, Singapore, Abu Dhabi and San Francisco. The partnership covers teaching, research, AI-powered case studies and agentic workflows, and includes plans to measure learning outcomes.

The pattern is consistent: universities would rather give students a managed, private tool than leave them on consumer accounts.

## 7. EDUCAUSE '26: higher ed moves from AI hype to AI plumbing

At the EDUCAUSE annual conference in Denver, EDUCAUSE President John O'Brien described how the conversation has changed four years into the ChatGPT era. "For those first few years, it was all about AI, AI, AI, and now it's AI in procurement, or AI for student retention," he said, as reported by GovTech on October 2.

O'Brien pointed to employer demand as the force reshaping curricula. Recent graduates face higher unemployment than the general population, and employers increasingly want candidates with AI experience. His core message was that AI is a technology that should serve an institution's mission, not become the mission.

## 8. Claude and ChatGPT go district-wide for teachers, not students

Anthropic made Claude for Teachers available to U.S. K-12 schools and districts as a free Enterprise offering on August 28. Administrators can bring all teachers and staff into one organization with single sign-on, role-based access and shared policies. The product includes teaching skills and links to academic standards in all 50 states. OpenAI's ChatGPT for Teachers offers similar district controls.

Neither product provides student accounts. That puts Anthropic and OpenAI on a different path from Google's student-facing Gemini rollout: they are betting that the safer way into K-12 runs through teachers first.

## 9. Research: AI that does the work can weaken learning

A Vox synthesis of cognitive research published this month draws a line that matters for every classroom policy. Students who used ChatGPT to complete work performed better at first but worse once the tool was taken away. Students who used AI to develop their reasoning outperformed those who handed off the task entirely.

That distinction is now showing up in product design, including Google's Guided Learning mode and teacher-controlled notebooks. The policy question for schools is shifting from "AI or no AI" to "which kind of AI use."

## 10. High school students say AI is useful, and a source of anxiety

At a California State University college fair, Bay Area high school juniors and seniors told EdSource that AI helps with their schoolwork but worries them. They raised concerns about what it will mean for their education, which careers will still exist, and how little control anyone seems to have over the technology.

It is a useful reminder for the adults making decisions. The students these policies affect are not resisting AI. They want to know what to learn so they are still employable when they graduate.
